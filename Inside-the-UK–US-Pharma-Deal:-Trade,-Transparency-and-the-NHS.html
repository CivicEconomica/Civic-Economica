Inside the UK–US Pharma-Deal:-Trade, Transparency and the NHS

By Ellie Nenguke · 16/1/2026 · Politics & Economics· 5 min read

Recent correspondence between the Science, Innovation and Technology Committee and the government has drawn attention to a structural issue in UK economic governance: how industrial strategy is financed and how its costs are distributed across the public sector. In writing to the Secretaries of State for Health and for Science and Technology, Committee Chair Dame Chi Onwurah has focused less on the principle of a UK–US pharmaceutical trade deal and more on the absence of clarity surrounding its fiscal and policy implications.

This distinction matters. Pharmaceutical trade agreements sit at the intersection of trade policy, public spending and industrial strategy. While such deals are often framed as growth-enhancing, their costs are rarely neutral. Instead, they tend to be absorbed by existing institutions most notably the NHS, raising questions about fiscal incidence rather than headline expenditure.

In her original letter, Dame Chi asked how negotiations with the United States might affect domestic pharmaceutical pricing and life sciences investment. The government declined to provide detail stating it could not offer a “running commentary” on negotiations. However, the subsequent appearance of pricing details in the Financial Times shortly afterwards complicates this justification. From an institutional perspective, this suggests a divergence between information disclosed to markets and that disclosed to Parliament, a pattern that can weaken democratic scrutiny without necessarily breaching formal rules.

The fiscal implications are non-trivial. NHS spending on medicines is estimated at around £19–20 billion per year representing roughly 10–11% of total NHS expenditure (Department of Health and Social Care). With NHS England’s total budget at approximately £180 billion for 2024–25 (NHS England), even modest increases in pharmaceutical prices or changes to rebate mechanisms can impose material pressure on service delivery. Importantly, these costs do not appear as new spending commitments; they are internalised within existing budgets.

This dynamic reflects a broader feature of contemporary industrial policy. Governments often pursue sector-specific growth strategies without allocating explicit funding envelopes, instead relying on quasi-fiscal mechanisms embedded within public services. In this case, the NHS functions as a residual claimant, absorbing costs generated by trade and industrial policy decisions made elsewhere in government.

Dame Chi’s follow-up letter presses the government for information on “how much the deal will cost and how much of that cost will be taken from the NHS’s already stretched budget.” Her concern reflects an underlying economic issue: when the costs of industrial strategy are opaque, they are more likely to be shifted onto institutions with limited bargaining power and high political salience.

This occurs against a backdrop of constrained public finances. Real-terms growth in UK health spending has averaged around 1.2% annually since 2019, significantly below the historical average of 3.7% (Institute for Fiscal Studies). At the same time, demand for healthcare continues to rise due to demographic change and increasing prevalence of chronic conditions. In such an environment, price-setting decisions in pharmaceuticals can crowd out spending elsewhere even if total health budgets remain formally unchanged.

The life sciences sector itself is economically significant, generating approximately £94 billion in annual turnover and supporting over 280,000 jobs (Office for Life Sciences). It is therefore understandable that the government presents pharmaceutical trade as central to its industrial strategy. However, investment confidence in this sector depends not only on favourable trade terms but on regulatory stability and predictable public procurement frameworks.

As Dame Chi Onwurah has argued, “the sector has lost confidence in successive governments’ ability to deliver on promises.” From a political economy standpoint, this loss of confidence reflects uncertainty over who ultimately bears the costs of policy commitments. Firms may benefit from enhanced market access but if pricing frameworks are subject to ad hoc adjustment through opaque deals, long-term investment planning becomes more difficult.

The government also declined to answer questions on life sciences funding on the grounds that it could not “pre-empt the Budget” despite the correspondence occurring after the Budget’s publication. This response illustrates a common feature of modern policymaking: the use of procedural constraints to limit scrutiny even when the formal justification is weak. While such practices may be administratively convenient, they risk undermining the role of parliamentary committees as mechanisms of accountability.

Crucially, this episode does not point to a failure of industrial strategy per se. Rather, it highlights the importance of institutional design. Effective industrial policy requires not only ambition but clarity about funding sources, cost allocation and accountability. Without this, policy risks becoming fragmented with growth objectives pursued in one domain and fiscal pressures managed reactively in another.

The broader lesson is that trade and industrial policy cannot be analysed in isolation from public finance. When governments leverage public services as instruments of economic strategy, transparency over fiscal incidence becomes essential. In the absence of such clarity, both public trust and policy effectiveness may be weakened.

As trade negotiations increasingly shape domestic economic outcomes, the challenge for policymakers lies in reconciling confidentiality with accountability. This case illustrates how the credibility of industrial strategy depends less on headline announcements and more on the institutional mechanisms through which costs and benefits are distributed across the state.
